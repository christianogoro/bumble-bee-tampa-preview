Kitchen Window Treatments: What Actually Works (and What Doesn't)

Kitchens are tough on window treatments. Moisture, grease, heat, and above-sink windows all create challenges. Here's what works, what fails, and what I actually recommend.

Kitchen Window Treatments: What Actually Works (and What Doesn’t)

A couple in Hyde Park renovated their kitchen last year. Beautiful project. Custom cabinetry, quartz counters, a six-burner range, the works. They spent something like on the renovation. Then they picked the window treatments themselves, without consulting anyone, and installed real wood plantation shutters above their kitchen sink.

I got the call eight months later. The shutters had warped. Not a little. The louvers were bowed, the frame was pulling away from the window casing, and there was visible mildew on the backside of the tilt bar. Eight months. In a kitchen.

“The contractor said wood shutters would be fine,” she told me. The contractor was wrong.

Kitchens are the hardest room in the house for window treatments. Bar none. You’ve got moisture from the dishwasher, steam from the stove, grease particles floating in the air every time you cook, food splatter near the sink, temperature swings from the oven, and in Tampa, intense UV coming through what’s usually the biggest window in the room. Most window treatments aren’t designed for any of those conditions, let alone all of them at once.

I’ve installed treatments in thousands of kitchens across the Tampa Bay area, and I’ll tell you straight up: most of what people put in their kitchens is wrong. Not ugly, necessarily. Just wrong for the environment. It’s going to fail, and it’s going to fail faster than the same product in any other room.

Here’s what actually works.

The Kitchen Window Challenge

Before we talk solutions, let’s talk about why kitchens destroy window treatments. Understanding the problem helps you make a smarter choice.

Moisture. Every time you boil water, run the dishwasher, or wash dishes in the sink, moisture goes into the air. In Tampa, where outdoor humidity averages 74% year-round, your kitchen’s humidity level can easily push above 80% during cooking. Real wood absorbs that moisture. It swells. It warps. It cracks when it dries back out. This cycle repeats daily, and each cycle does a little more damage.

Grease. When you cook with oil, fat, or butter, microscopic grease particles become airborne. They settle on everything, including your window treatments. On smooth surfaces (composite, vinyl, faux wood), grease wipes right off. On fabric, it absorbs and builds up over time. After a year, a fabric valance near the stove will have a yellowish film and a faintly stale smell. After two years, it’s genuinely unpleasant.

Heat. If you have a window near your stove or oven (and many Tampa kitchen layouts put a window above the sink, which is often adjacent to the cooktop), heat exposure is constant. Some fabrics can degrade, discolor, or become brittle from repeated heat exposure.

The above-sink window. This is the most common kitchen window, and it’s the hardest one to treat. It’s directly above a water source. It usually gets splashed. It’s hard to reach (you have to lean over the sink and counter). And in most homes, it’s the primary source of natural light in the kitchen, so you don’t want to block it entirely.

What Works: The Top Kitchen Options

Composite Plantation Shutters

This is what I put in that Hyde Park kitchen after the wood shutters failed. Composite shutters look nearly identical to real wood, but they’re engineered from a moisture-resistant polymer core with a painted or wrapped finish. They will not warp, swell, crack, or mildew. Period.

For kitchen use, composite shutters are close to perfect. They handle moisture without flinching. Grease wipes off with a damp cloth and mild soap. They don’t absorb odors. They’re easy to clean because the louvers tilt open for access and the panels open for full window access. And they look fantastic.

the durability pays off over time.

Best brands for kitchens: Hunter Douglas Palm Beach (the gold standard for moisture resistance), Norman Woodlure (excellent quality at a slightly more accessible tier), and Alta Composite (solid mid-range option).

Above-sink performance: Excellent. The rigid frame and louver design means they stay put and operate smoothly even with occasional splashing. I’ve installed hundreds of composite shutters above kitchen sinks and I’ve never had a moisture-related callback. Not once.

Faux Wood Blinds

If shutters are outside your home, faux wood blinds are the kitchen workhorse. They’re made from PVC or a composite material that’s completely waterproof. They look like real wood (from a distance, at least), they’re easy to clean, and they’re quality.

The 2-inch or 2.5-inch slat size works best in kitchens. It gives you good light control without looking too bulky. Avoid 1-inch mini blinds in kitchens. They’re harder to clean (grease gets trapped between the thin slats and they’re tedious to wipe) and they look cheap, especially in a renovated kitchen.

Best brands: Hunter Douglas EverWood is the premium option and genuinely looks like real wood. Graber Traditions is solid. For practical options, Bali and Levolor faux wood blinds from Lowe’s or Home Depot are adequate, though the quality of the finish and the smoothness of operation aren’t in the same league as Hunter Douglas or Graber.

Above-sink performance: Very good. Moisture isn’t a problem. The one consideration is that standard cord-operated faux wood blinds above a deep sink can be annoying to reach. Cordless lift or motorized options solve this but add cost.

Best for: Any kitchen window, especially above the sink where moisture resistance matters most.

Roller Shades in Moisture-Resistant Fabric

Roller shades are the modern, minimalist option for kitchens. They sit clean and flat against the window, take up almost no visual space, and can be made from materials that are ideal for kitchen environments.

The key is the fabric. For kitchens, you want a PVC or vinyl-coated fabric that’s wipeable. Some manufacturers offer “kitchen-rated” roller fabrics that are specifically designed to resist moisture and grease. Solar screen fabrics (5% openness) are also a good kitchen choice because they block UV and heat while letting you see your backyard, and they’re easy to clean.

Avoid fabric roller shades with a soft, porous weave in kitchens. They’ll absorb grease and moisture the same way curtains would.

Best brands: Insolroll makes some of the best roller shades in the industry, with fabrics specifically rated for high-humidity environments. Hunter Douglas Designer Roller Shades are premium and come in wipeable fabrics. Graber roller shades offer a good mid-range option.

Above-sink performance: Good, with one caveat. Standard roller shades use a spring or clutch mechanism at the top of the window. When the shade is down, the bottom hangs freely. In an above-sink location, the bottom edge can get splashed or catch water. Inside-mounting the shade (tucked inside the window frame) helps keep it farther from the sink edge.

Moisture-Resistant Roman Shades

I include this with a big asterisk. Traditional Roman shades in a standard fabric are a terrible kitchen choice. They’re fabric sponges that absorb grease, moisture, and odors. But there are now Roman-style shades made with moisture-resistant materials (coated fabrics, PVC-backed liners) that can work in kitchens.

Hunter Douglas Design Studio and Graber’s Roman shade lines offer moisture-resistant fabric options. They give you the soft, layered look of a Roman shade with better kitchen durability. They’re not waterproof, though. They handle steam and ambient moisture, but direct water contact will still cause problems.

Best for: Kitchens where the window isn’t directly above the sink, or where the homeowner prioritizes the Roman shade aesthetic and is willing to accept slightly higher maintenance.

What Doesn’t Work: Skip These in the Kitchen

I’ve learned these lessons from callbacks, replacements, and that look on a homeowner’s face when they realize their expensive treatment is failing after less than a year.

Real Wood Shutters and Blinds

The Hyde Park story says it all. Real wood has no place in a kitchen, period. It doesn’t matter how well it’s sealed, stained, or finished. Wood is hygroscopic. It absorbs and releases moisture based on the environment. In a kitchen, especially a Florida kitchen with 74% baseline outdoor humidity, real wood will warp. Maybe not in three months. Maybe not in six. But within a year or two, you’ll see it.

Exception: If your kitchen window is nowhere near the sink, stove, or dishwasher, and you have excellent ventilation (a powerful range hood vented to the outside), real wood might survive. But why risk it when composite looks almost identical and is guaranteed to last?

Fabric Valances and Curtains

Fabric valances above the kitchen sink were the default in the 1990s and early 2000s. They’re still in a lot of Tampa kitchens. They need to go.

Fabric near a stove or cooktop absorbs grease. The smell builds up over time. The color changes (usually yellowing). They’re hard to wash because most are sewn onto a rod pocket and don’t easily detach. And the ones near a gas or open-flame cooktop are a genuine fire hazard. The National Fire Protection Association lists kitchen curtains and drapes as a leading source of kitchen fires caused by fabrics igniting from a stovetop.

If you love the look of a soft treatment in your kitchen, use it on a window that’s far from the cooking and water zones. A breakfast nook window with a light linen curtain panel is fine. A curtain above the stove is not.

Cellular Shades (In Most Kitchen Situations)

This might be surprising because I recommend cellular shades for almost every other room. But kitchens are the one place I hesitate.

The honeycomb structure that makes cellular shades great for insulation also traps moisture and grease. Steam from the stove rises, hits the shade, and condenses inside the cells. Over time, this can cause mildew, discoloration, and a musty smell that’s almost impossible to eliminate because you can’t get inside the cells to clean them.

Some manufacturers offer moisture-resistant cellular fabric (Hunter Douglas Duette with the “Duolite” moisture-resistant fabric, for example), and these perform better. But even the moisture-resistant versions aren’t ideal directly above a sink or adjacent to a cooktop.

Exception: If your kitchen window is well away from moisture and heat sources, cellular shades are fine. They’re just not my first recommendation for the typical above-sink kitchen window.

Woven Wood and Natural Shades

Bamboo, jute, reed, grass cloth. These look gorgeous. They also absorb moisture, trap grease, and can develop mold in high-humidity environments. In a Florida kitchen, woven wood shades are a ticking time bomb. Hard pass.

The Above-Sink Window: Solving the #1 Kitchen Window

Let’s zoom in on this because it’s the most common question I get about kitchen windows. “What do I put above the sink?”

The answer depends on what you prioritize:

If you want maximum durability and circumstances allow: Composite shutters. They’ll last 20+ years in that position, they look premium, and the louvers give you precise light control. You can tilt them open while doing dishes for light and tilt them closed for privacy at night. Water splashes on them? Wipe and forget.

If you want a clean, modern look: A roller shade in a wipeable fabric. Inside-mount it so it sits within the window frame, out of reach of most splashing. When you’re not at the sink, roll it down for UV protection and privacy. When you’re washing dishes and want light, roll it up and it disappears into the headrail.

If you want the most quality option that actually works: Faux wood blinds. A quality set of 2.5-inch faux wood blinds above the sink will on window size and brand, and will perform well for 5-10 years.

If you want no treatment at all: Honestly, this is valid. Some kitchen windows, especially those facing a private backyard, don’t need a treatment. If privacy isn’t an issue and you don’t mind the direct sunlight, leaving the window bare is a perfectly fine choice. It’s not lazy. It’s intentional. I’d rather see an untreated window than a treatment that doesn’t belong there.

Real Talk About Kitchen Ventilation

I’ll get slightly off-topic here because it matters to your window treatments. If your kitchen doesn’t have a range hood that vents to the outside, every window treatment in the room is working harder than it should be.

A recirculating range hood (the kind with a charcoal filter that pushes air back into the kitchen) does almost nothing for moisture and grease. It catches some of the big stuff, but the fine grease particles and steam still go everywhere.

A ducted range hood that vents to the exterior removes moisture and grease at the source. If you’re renovating your kitchen and considering new window treatments at the same time, invest in proper ventilation. Your treatments will last significantly longer, your kitchen will smell better, and you’ll have fewer moisture issues throughout the room.

Most Tampa homes built before 2000 have recirculating hoods or no hood at all. If that’s your home, lean hard toward the most moisture-resistant treatments: composite shutters or faux wood blinds. They can handle the extra moisture load.

The Hyde Park Resolution

We replaced those warped wood shutters with Hunter Douglas Palm Beach composite shutters. Same look, same style, same color. From three feet away, you can’t tell they’re not real wood. But they’re engineered for exactly this kind of environment.

The homeowner told me she actually prefers the composite now because they feel lighter to operate. The louvers tilt smoothly, the panels swing easily, and she can splash water on them when she’s rinsing vegetables without flinching. She couldn’t do that with the wood ones, and that hesitation had made the shutters feel more like an obstacle than a benefit.

The renovation contractor stopped by and asked where we got the “new wood shutters.” When she told him they were composite, he was genuinely surprised. He now recommends composite for kitchen installations. Lesson learned, albeit an expensive one.

Picking the Right Treatment for Your Kitchen

Your kitchen probably has 1-3 windows, and they might need different treatments. The window above the sink needs maximum moisture resistance. The window near the breakfast table might be more about style and light control. The window facing the neighbor’s house might be about privacy.

Don’t feel like every kitchen window needs the same treatment. Mixing can work well as long as the colors and overall style coordinate. Composite shutters above the sink, a roller shade on the side window, and nothing on the small transom is a perfectly valid combination.

A free in-home consultation lets us look at your specific kitchen, assess moisture and light conditions at each window, and show you product samples right there in the room. The lighting in your kitchen is different from a showroom, and seeing samples against your cabinets and counters ensures you make a choice you’ll love.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the best window treatments for Tampa Bay homes?

The best window treatments for Tampa Bay homes are faux wood blinds (value), cellular shades (energy efficiency), and composite shutters (durability and resale value), chosen for their resistance to Florida’s humidity, UV exposure, and heat. Hunter Douglas, Graber, Norman, and Bali cover every style and need and style, with composite materials recommended for Florida’s climate. For a personalized recommendation based on your specific windows and sun exposure, Bumble Bee Blinds offers free in-home consultations with real product samples.

How long do window treatments last in Florida?

Window treatments typically last 5-25 years in Florida when properly installed and maintained. Florida’s intense UV and humidity can shorten the lifespan of lower-quality materials by 1-3 years compared to northern climates. Choosing humidity-resistant materials like composite, faux wood, or vinyl and investing in quality brands significantly extends product life in the Tampa Bay climate.

What window treatments work best in Florida’s humidity?

Composite plantation shutters, faux wood blinds, and vinyl treatments work best in Florida’s humidity because they are engineered to resist moisture absorption, warping, and mildew. Avoid real wood in bathrooms and kitchens where humidity is highest. For dry, air-conditioned rooms, real wood treatments are fine because your AC controls the ambient moisture. In the Tampa Bay area, faux wood is the most popular choice for its humidity resistance and natural wood appearance.

What are the best window treatments for a kitchen?

The best window treatments for a kitchen are faux wood blinds, composite shutters, or easy-clean roller shades because they resist grease, moisture, and cooking splatter while being easy to wipe down. Kitchens need treatments that can handle humidity from cooking and occasional splashes. Avoid fabric Roman shades near the stove or sink. Faux wood and composite materials clean with a simple damp cloth. In Tampa Bay homes, also consider easy-clean materials that resist both cooking moisture and Florida’s ambient humidity, which together create a tough environment for lower-quality products for this room.

Do I need blackout window treatments in my kitchen?

Blackout window treatments are not necessary for a kitchen. Kitchens benefit from natural light for cooking and entertaining, so light-filtering or adjustable treatments work better than blackout options. The most effective blackout options are cellular shades with blackout fabric, roller shades with room-darkening material, or plantation shutters with tight louver closure. In Florida, blackout treatments in bedrooms also provide significant comfort benefits by blocking the intense morning or afternoon sun.
