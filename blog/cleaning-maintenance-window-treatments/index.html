How to Clean and Maintain Your Window Treatments (Without Ruining Them)

Product-specific cleaning guide for blinds, shades, shutters, and drapes. Florida-specific tips for mold, humidity, pollen, and UV damage prevention.

How to Clean and Maintain Your Window Treatments (Without Ruining Them)

I visited a homeowner in Carrollwood last year who asked me to look at her cellular shades because “the color seems off.” She’d had white honeycomb shades in her living room for five years. Never cleaned them. Not once. When I asked her what color they were originally, she said white. They were gray. Not a subtle off-white. Gray. Five years of Tampa dust, pollen, and cooking residue had built up so gradually that she didn’t even notice the color shift until her neighbor got new white shades and she saw the difference.

Here’s the uncomfortable truth: most people never clean their window treatments. They clean their floors, their counters, their bathrooms, their windows. But the things hanging on those windows? Ignored until they’re visibly dirty, damaged, or being replaced.

The good news is that cleaning window treatments isn’t hard. The bad news is that every product type requires a different approach, and using the wrong method on the wrong product can cause real damage. I’ve seen people run fabric Roman shades through a washing machine and pull out a wadded mess of twisted fabric. I’ve seen someone spray Windex on faux wood blinds and strip the finish. These aren’t products you can just throw in the wash or hose down in the driveway.

So let me walk through every product type, what works, what doesn’t, and what will ruin them.

Blinds (Faux Wood, Real Wood, Aluminum, Vinyl)

Blinds are the most common window treatment in Tampa homes, and they’re also the most satisfying to clean because the difference is immediate.

Regular dusting (monthly). Close the blinds so the slats are flat. Run a microfiber cloth, dusting glove, or the brush attachment on your vacuum across each slat. Work from top to bottom so the dust falls down, not onto slats you’ve already cleaned. Flip the blinds to the other direction and repeat. That’s it. Five minutes

Deep cleaning (every 6-12 months). For faux wood and vinyl blinds, dampen a microfiber cloth with warm water and a tiny drop of dish soap. Wipe each slat individually, top and bottom. Dry with a clean cloth. Don’t soak them or let water pool in the headrail mechanism.

For real wood blinds, be much more careful with moisture. Use a barely damp cloth and dry immediately. Wood absorbs water, and in Tampa’s humidity, wet wood blinds can warp or develop water spots. A furniture polish like Murphy Oil Soap (diluted) works well for real wood every few months.

Aluminum mini blinds can handle more water. You can actually take them down, lay them in the bathtub with warm soapy water, scrub gently with a soft brush, rinse, and hang to dry. This is the most thorough cleaning method and works well for kitchen or bathroom blinds that have grease or moisture buildup.

What will damage them: Harsh chemicals, abrasive scrubbers, pressure washing, leaving them soaking for extended periods (the cords and mechanisms rust or degrade), and any cleaning product with bleach on colored or wood blinds.

Plantation Shutters

Shutters are the easiest window treatment to maintain, which is one of the many reasons I’m a fan.

Regular cleaning (monthly). Dust with a microfiber cloth or dry Swiffer pad. Open the louvers flat, wipe each one, then wipe the frame. Because shutters are a hard surface with flat planes, dusting takes about two minutes The dust doesn’t embed into fabric or hide in honeycomb cells. It just sits on top.

Deep cleaning (every 6-12 months). Dampen a cloth with warm water and a drop of mild soap. Wipe down the louvers, stiles, and frame. Dry with a clean cloth. For composite shutters (like Hunter Douglas NewStyle or Norman Woodlure), you can be a bit more liberal with moisture. They’re engineered to handle humidity. For real wood shutters, use minimal moisture and dry thoroughly.

Hinge maintenance. Once a year, check the tension on each shutter panel’s tilt rod. If the louvers are sagging or won’t stay in the position you set them, the tension screw on the hinge just needs a quarter turn with a screwdriver. This takes 30 seconds and keeps the shutters functioning properly for decades.

What will damage them: Power washing (yes, someone asked me this), abrasive cleaners, excessive water on real wood shutters, and WD-40 on the hinges (it attracts dust and gums up over time; use a dry silicone lubricant if the hinges squeak).

Cellular (Honeycomb) Shades

Cellular shades are the trickiest to clean because of their honeycomb construction. Dust and debris get trapped inside the cells, and you can’t just reach in there with a cloth.

Regular dusting (monthly). Use the brush attachment on your vacuum on the lowest suction setting. Gently run it across the face of the shade, working horizontally along the pleats. Don’t press hard or you’ll crush the cells. Alternatively, a can of compressed air (the kind you use on keyboard electronics) works surprisingly well for blowing dust out of the cells.

Spot cleaning. For individual stains or marks, use a clean white cloth dampened with lukewarm water and a tiny amount of mild detergent. Blot the stain gently. Don’t rub. Rubbing pushes the stain deeper into the fabric and can damage the pleat structure. Let the area air dry completely before raising the shade.

Deep cleaning. This is where it gets tricky. Some cellular shades can be submerged in a bathtub of lukewarm water with mild detergent, gently agitated, rinsed, and hung to air dry. But this depends entirely on the fabric and manufacturer. Hunter Douglas Duette shades, for example, can handle a gentle bath. Some other brands cannot. Always check the manufacturer’s care instructions before submerging.

That Carrollwood homeowner’s shades? We tried compressed air first, which helped. Then a gentle bath in the tub. They came out noticeably lighter but still not the bright white they’d been five years earlier. Some staining from years of accumulated grime was permanent. She ended up replacing them. If she’d dusted them monthly, they’d still be on her windows.

What will damage them: Machine washing, wringing or twisting, steam cleaning (the heat can melt adhesive in the cells), harsh chemicals, and using high suction on the vacuum.

Roller Shades

Roller shades are relatively easy to maintain, but the approach depends on the fabric.

Regular dusting (monthly). Lower the shade fully and use a lint roller or the brush attachment on your vacuum. For textured fabrics, the vacuum works better. For smooth fabrics, the lint roller is faster.

Spot cleaning. Dampen a cloth with warm water and blot the spot. For stubborn marks on vinyl or PVC-coated roller shades, a magic eraser (melamine sponge) works well but test it on an inconspicuous area first. Some finishes don’t react well to melamine.

Deep cleaning. Unroll the shade completely. Lay it flat on a clean surface (a clean garage floor works, or drape it over a clean table). Wipe the entire surface with a damp cloth and mild soap. Wipe again with clean water to remove soap residue. Let it air dry completely before rolling it back up. Never roll up a damp shade. The moisture trapped in the roll will cause mold and permanently warp the fabric.

What will damage them: Rolling them up while wet, using bleach on colored fabrics, scrubbing textured surfaces aggressively, and forcing a stuck spring mechanism (if the shade won’t retract smoothly, the spring tension needs adjusting, not forcing).

Roman Shades

Roman shades are fabric, and fabric needs the most careful handling.

Regular maintenance (monthly). Vacuum with the upholstery attachment on low suction. Pay attention to the fold lines where the fabric gathers, because that’s where dust collects most.

Spot cleaning. Blot stains with a clean white cloth and cold water. For stubborn stains, a fabric spot cleaner designed for upholstery is safe for most Roman shade fabrics. Always test in a hidden area first (the back or inside a fold).

Professional cleaning is recommended for deep cleaning Roman shades. The fabric, lining, boning (the rods that create the folds), and lift mechanisms all react differently to water and cleaning solutions. A professional drapery cleaner knows how to clean the fabric without shrinking it, distorting the folds, or damaging the lining.

I had a homeowner in Hyde Park who machine-washed her unlined Roman shades. The fabric shrank unevenly, the folds no longer aligned with the lift cords, and the shade hung crooked. She had to replace all four shades. Professional cleaning would have typically comes with The replacements cost each.

What will damage them: Machine washing (unless the manufacturer specifically says it’s safe), ironing directly on the face fabric, bleach, and hanging them wet (the weight of the water stretches the fabric).

Drapery and Curtains

Drapes and curtains vary wildly by fabric, so the cleaning method depends on what you’ve got.

Lightweight unlined curtains (cotton, polyester, linen blends) can often be machine washed on a gentle cycle in cold water and hung to dry. Check the care label. Some polyester curtains are genuinely machine-washable and come out looking fine. Others shrink or wrinkle permanently.

Lined drapes should be dry cleaned. The face fabric and the lining are different materials that shrink at different rates. Machine washing lined drapes almost always results in puckering where the two layers pull apart. Professional dry cleaning preserves the drape and keeps the layers aligned.

Velvet, silk, and specialty fabric drapes need professional care, period. Don’t attempt to clean these at home.

Between cleanings, vacuum drapes with the upholstery attachment every few months. Give them a good shake to dislodge dust. If they’re near the kitchen, they’ll absorb cooking odors. A fabric refresher spray (like Febreze) is fine between cleanings for odor, but it’s not a substitute for actual cleaning.

What will damage them: Hot water on natural fibers, machine washing lined drapes, iron-on fabric without checking the heat setting, and leaving drapes bunched up on the floor (the weight creates permanent creases).

Florida-Specific Maintenance Concerns

Living in Tampa means dealing with cleaning challenges that homeowners in dryer climates never face.

Mold and mildew are the biggest Florida-specific concern. Any fabric treatment in a humid environment can develop mold, especially in rooms with poor air circulation. Bathrooms, laundry rooms, and guest rooms that stay closed up for weeks are the highest risk. The first sign is usually a musty smell. The second is small dark spots on the fabric.

Prevention is everything. Keep your AC running consistently (even when you’re away or on vacation, set it to 78 and let it dehumidify). Use exhaust fans in bathrooms. Don’t leave wet towels near fabric window treatments. If you’re a snowbird and your Tampa home sits empty during summer, set the thermostat to 76-78 and consider a standalone dehumidifier. I’ve walked into snowbird homes in September where the Roman shades were spotted with mold because the house sat at 85 degrees and 80% humidity for four months.

If mold does appear, a solution of one part white vinegar to three parts water, applied with a spray bottle and blotted with a clean cloth, kills most surface mold on fabric. For severe mold on cellular shades or Roman shades, professional cleaning or replacement may be the only option.

Pollen season in Tampa runs roughly from February through May. Oak pollen is the worst offender. It’s that yellow dust that covers every car, every porch, and every window sill. If your windows are open during pollen season (which they are, because Tampa has about six gorgeous weeks of weather in spring), that pollen is settling on your window treatments.

The fix is simple: increase your dusting frequency during pollen season. Monthly becomes biweekly. A quick vacuum pass over your shades and a wipe-down of your shutters every two weeks during peak pollen keeps it from building up into a visible layer.

Salt air affects homes near the coast and bay. If you’re in Shore Acres, Davis Islands, or along Bayshore Boulevard, salt air can corrode metal components on blinds and accelerate the breakdown of certain fabrics. Wipe down metal headrails and tilt mechanisms with a damp cloth occasionally to remove salt residue.

The most important item on this list is the monthly dust. If you do nothing else, dust your window treatments once a month. It takes less than an hour for an entire house, and it prevents 90% of the buildup that leads to permanent discoloration and premature replacement.

When Cleaning Won’t Save Them

Sometimes a window treatment is past the point of cleaning. Here’s when to stop trying and start shopping.

Cellular shades with permanent discoloration that doesn’t respond to cleaning. Fabric shades with widespread mold that keeps coming back after treatment. Blinds with mechanisms that stick, skip, or don’t hold position. Roller shades with spring mechanisms that won’t maintain tension. Any product where the fabric has become brittle, cracked, or structurally weak from UV exposure.

Cleaning maintains products that are in reasonable condition. It doesn’t resurrect products that have reached the end of their functional life.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I clean my window treatments?

Most window treatments can be cleaned with a microfiber cloth or light vacuuming with a brush attachment. For blinds and shutters, wipe each slat with a damp cloth. For fabric shades, use a handheld vacuum on low suction. Avoid soaking real wood treatments. In Tampa Bay, clean your treatments every 2-3 months to prevent dust and pollen buildup that accumulates faster in Florida’s climate. Plantation shutters are the easiest to maintain, requiring only a quick damp wipe.

When should I replace my window treatments?

Replace your window treatments when you notice visible warping, yellowing, broken slats, frayed cords, or mechanisms that no longer operate smoothly. Most blinds last 5-8 years, cellular shades 7-10 years, and plantation shutters 15-25+ years. In Florida, cheap vinyl and aluminum treatments may need replacement sooner due to UV degradation and humidity damage. If your treatments are builder-grade originals from when your home was built, they are almost certainly overdue for an upgrade.

What are the best window treatments for Tampa Bay homes?

The best window treatments for Tampa Bay homes are faux wood blinds (value), cellular shades (energy efficiency), and composite shutters (durability and resale value), chosen for their resistance to Florida’s humidity, UV exposure, and heat. Hunter Douglas, Graber, Norman, and Bali cover every style and need and style, with composite materials recommended for Florida’s climate. For a personalized recommendation based on your specific windows and sun exposure, Bumble Bee Blinds offers free in-home consultations with real product samples.

How long do window treatments last in Florida?

Window treatments typically last 5-25 years in Florida when properly installed and maintained. Florida’s intense UV and humidity can shorten the lifespan of lower-quality materials by 1-3 years compared to northern climates. Choosing humidity-resistant materials like composite, faux wood, or vinyl and investing in quality brands significantly extends product life in the Tampa Bay climate.

What window treatments work best in Florida’s humidity?

Composite plantation shutters, faux wood blinds, and vinyl treatments work best in Florida’s humidity because they are engineered to resist moisture absorption, warping, and mildew. Avoid real wood in bathrooms and kitchens where humidity is highest. For dry, air-conditioned rooms, real wood treatments are fine because your AC controls the ambient moisture. In the Tampa Bay area, faux wood is the most popular choice for its humidity resistance and natural wood appearance.
