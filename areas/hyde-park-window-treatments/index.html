Window Treatments for Hyde Park Homes: Historic Charm Meets Modern Function

How Hyde Park homeowners are adding modern window treatments to historic Tampa homes without sacrificing character. Shutters, Roman shades, and more.

The homeowner was almost in tears. She’d just bought a 1912 Craftsman bungalow two blocks from Hyde Park Village and wanted plantation shutters installed. The first company she called took one look at the tall, narrow windows with their original cypress frames and told her they couldn’t do it. “Non-standard dimensions,” they said. “You’d need to replace the windows first.”

Replace the original 1912 cypress windows in a home on Tampa’s historic registry. That was their solution.

She found us through a neighbor’s recommendation, and I’m glad she did. Those windows didn’t need replacing. They needed someone who knows how to work with old homes. We custom-fabricated shutters that fit those openings perfectly, with mounting hardware designed for plaster walls and century-old wood frames. The shutters went in clean, the windows stayed original, and the home kept every bit of its character.

That’s the thing about working in Hyde Park. You can’t treat these homes like they rolled off a production line in 2015.

Hyde Park’s Architectural DNA

Hyde Park is Tampa’s oldest and most architecturally significant residential neighborhood. Walking these streets feels like flipping through a textbook on early 20th-century American home design.

You’ve got Craftsman bungalows with their wide front porches, tapered columns, and exposed rafter tails. Colonial Revival homes with symmetrical facades and double-hung windows. Mediterranean Revivals with stucco walls, barrel tile roofs, and those gorgeous arched openings. There’s even a handful of Prairie Style and Tudor Revival homes scattered through the neighborhood.

What they all have in common is age. Most of these homes were built between 1900 and 1940. And with that age comes window characteristics you won’t find in any other Tampa neighborhood.

Tall, narrow windows. The standard window in a Hyde Park Craftsman is taller and narrower than modern windows. Instead of the 36-by-60-inch openings you see in new construction, you’re often dealing with 28-by-72 or even 24-by-84 proportions. Standard-width blinds and shades simply don’t come in these sizes off the shelf.

Original wood frames. Many of the homes on the historic registry still have their original window frames, often cypress or heart pine. These frames have settled, shifted, and been painted over multiple times. The opening that was 28 inches wide when the house was built might be 27.75 on one side and 28.25 on the other.

Transoms. A lot of the larger Hyde Park homes have transom windows above the main windows, especially in living rooms and dining rooms. Before AC was common in Florida, these transoms provided ventilation. Now they’re decorative, but they still need treatment. And they’re often ten or twelve feet off the floor.

Arched and specialty shapes. The Mediterranean Revivals in Hyde Park love their arches. Palladian windows, half-round tops, eyebrow arches. Every one of them needs a custom template and custom fabrication.

The Historic Registry Factor

Here’s something that catches a lot of new Hyde Park homeowners off guard: if your home is on the local historic registry, there are rules about what you can and can’t change on the exterior. Window treatments are primarily interior, so they typically don’t fall under historic review. But anything visible from the street can potentially trigger questions.

What does this mean in practice? It means you want window treatments that look appropriate from the outside. A bright red cellular shade visible through the front window of a 1920 Colonial Revival is technically allowed, but it’s going to look wrong. And your neighbors will have opinions.

I always recommend that Hyde Park homeowners choose products with a clean, neutral street-side appearance. Plantation shutters are ideal for this because the louver profile looks consistent and classic from the exterior. White or off-white cellular shades also work well. What you want to avoid are products with visible cords, uneven hems, or colors that clash with the home’s exterior palette.

What Works Best in Hyde Park Homes

After years of working in this neighborhood, I have strong opinions about what belongs in these homes and what doesn’t. Here’s the honest breakdown:

ProductHyde Park FitBest Used For
Plantation ShuttersExcellentLiving rooms, dining rooms, front-facing windows
Roman ShadesExcellentBedrooms, studies, bathrooms
Cellular ShadesVery GoodBedrooms, back-of-house rooms
Motorized ShadesVery GoodTransoms, high windows, hard-to-reach spots
Custom DraperyGoodFormal living rooms, paired with sheers or shades
Roller ShadesFairKitchens, utility rooms
Vertical BlindsPoorAvoid in historic homes entirely
Faux Wood BlindsPoorToo casual for most Hyde Park architecture

Let me explain some of these choices.

Plantation shutters are the gold standard here. A well-crafted shutter in a Hyde Park home looks like it was installed when the house was built. The architectural weight of a shutter matches the substantial feel of these older homes. Thin, flimsy blinds look like an afterthought. Shutters look intentional.

I typically recommend Norman or Hunter Douglas for Hyde Park installations. Norman’s Woodlure and PerfectFit lines handle the humidity well, and their custom sizing accommodates the non-standard openings you find in nearly every home here. For homes with severe humidity issues, composite materials outperform real wood every time in Tampa’s climate.

Roman shades bring softness without sacrificing style. Historic homes can feel heavy, with all the plaster, dark wood trim, and tile. Roman shades in a textured linen or cotton fabric lighten a room without making it feel modern or out of place. I use them frequently in Hyde Park bedrooms and home offices where shutters might feel too formal.

Motorized shades for transoms are almost mandatory. Nobody is climbing a ladder every day to adjust the shade on a 12-foot-high transom window. Hunter Douglas PowerView or Graber’s motorized systems let you control those high windows from your phone or set them on an automatic schedule. It sounds like a luxury, but in a home where half the windows are out of reach, it’s really just practical.

Vertical blinds and cheap faux wood blinds need to stay out of Hyde Park. I know that sounds snobby, but these homes deserve better. A set of vertical blinds on a sliding door in a 1925 Craftsman is an architectural crime. If you have a sliding glass door in a Hyde Park home (many added them during mid-century renovations), consider vertical cellular shades or panel track shades instead. They cover the same opening with far more style.

The 10-Foot Arched Window Project

One of my favorite Hyde Park projects was a home on Swann Avenue, a beautifully maintained Colonial Revival that the owner had lived in for 22 years. Robert had tried three times over the years to get window treatments for his front living room, which had two 10-foot arched windows flanking the fireplace. Each window was about 42 inches wide with a half-round arch at the top.

Every company he’d worked with either wanted to put a standard rectangular shutter on the lower portion and leave the arch bare, or they quoted him a price that he felt was unreasonable for the product quality they were offering.

When I walked through the house, I could see why those windows had been bare for 22 years. They’re genuinely challenging. The arches aren’t perfect semicircles. They’re slightly elliptical, which means a standard sunburst fan won’t fit. Each arch needed to be individually templated.

We went with Norman plantation shutters in a custom white finish to match his existing trim. The lower rectangular sections have standard operating louvers. The arched tops have fixed louvers set at 45 degrees, following the exact curve of each individual arch. Because yes, the two arches were slightly different from each other. The left one had a 21-inch rise and the right had a 20.5-inch rise. Half an inch matters.

The installation took a full day for just those two windows. Between the templating, the custom fabrication timeline of about five weeks, and the careful mounting into the original plaster walls, this was a true craft project.

Robert called me the next week and asked if we could do the rest of the house. We ended up putting plantation shutters on every window in the home over the course of three months. Sixteen windows total, no two exactly the same size.

Installation Realities in Historic Hyde Park Homes

Working in Hyde Park requires a different skill set than working in newer neighborhoods. Here’s what professional installation actually looks like in these homes.

Plaster walls require special techniques. You can’t just use drywall anchors in plaster. The material is harder, more brittle, and often has wooden lath behind it. Drilling into plaster without the right bit and technique will crack it, and plaster repair in a historic home is expensive. We use masonry bits at low speed and specialized anchors designed for plaster-over-lath construction.

Window frames aren’t square. I measure every window in a Hyde Park home at three points across the width and three points along the height. The smallest measurement determines the product size for inside-mount installations. Assuming a window is the same width at the top and bottom will get you a product that either doesn’t fit or has visible gaps.

Original hardware matters. Some Hyde Park windows still have their original brass or iron hardware. Locks, lifts, sash weights. The right installer knows how to work around these features without removing or damaging them. If your installer’s first instinct is to rip out the original window hardware to make the blinds fit, find a different installer.

Weight considerations. Plaster walls in older homes can handle weight, but the mounting needs to go into the studs or the wooden lath, not just the plaster surface. A full set of hardwood plantation shutters on a large window is heavy. Twenty, thirty, even forty pounds depending on the window size. That weight needs to be properly distributed across solid mounting points.

Pairing Window Treatments with Hyde Park’s Character

One thing I always discuss with Hyde Park homeowners is how their window treatments will interact with the existing architectural details in their homes. Crown molding, picture rails, chair rails, wainscoting, original wood floors. These elements create a visual context that your window treatments need to complement.

A few principles I follow:

If the home has dark wood trim (common in Craftsman bungalows), I lean toward white or cream shutters for contrast, or match the stain exactly with a wood-tone shutter. The middle ground of “sort of matching” always looks off.

If the home has painted trim (typical in Colonial Revivals), the window treatment color should match the trim, not the wall. This creates a cohesive frame around each window.

For Mediterranean Revivals with textured stucco walls and wrought-iron details, Roman shades in earthy tones or warm neutrals reinforce the architectural style. Cool grays and blues can feel disconnected from the warmth of these homes.

And across all styles, the hardware matters. Brushed nickel looks right in a Craftsman. Oil-rubbed bronze works in a Mediterranean. Chrome feels modern and works in renovated spaces but can feel out of place in an untouched historic interior.

Energy Efficiency in Older Homes

Here’s a practical benefit that gets overlooked. Hyde Park’s older homes typically have single-pane windows. Some have been upgraded to double-pane, but many haven’t. That single pane of glass is basically a direct thermal connection between your air-conditioned interior and Tampa’s 95-degree summer heat.

Cellular shades, with their honeycomb construction, create an insulating air pocket between the window and your room. Hunter Douglas Duette shades, for example, are available in single, double, or triple cell configurations, with increasing insulation as you go up. In a home with 15 or 20 single-pane windows, the comfort benefits from cellular shades can genuinely reduce your monthly electric bill.

I had a client on Plant Avenue who tracked her electric bill for six months before and after installing Duette shades on all her south-facing and west-facing windows. Her summer electric bills dropped noticeably12%. In dollar terms, that was roughly per month. The shades paid for themselves over several years, and in the meantime, every room was more comfortable.
