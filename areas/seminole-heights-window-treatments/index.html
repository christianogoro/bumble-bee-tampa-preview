Window Treatments for Seminole Heights: Character Homes Deserve Better

I measured a 1926 Craftsman bungalow on Osborne Avenue last October that had seventeen windows, and not a single one of them was the same size. Not one. The homeowner stood there watching me record measurements with this knowing grin on his face, like he’d been waiting for someone to see what he’d been dealing with. His kitchen window was 42 inches wide. The one six feet away in the dining room? Thirty-seven and three-eighths. The living room had a beautiful triple-set with a wide center pane flanked by two narrow sidelights, and the whole assembly was slightly wider on the left than the right because the house had settled over the past century.

Welcome to window treatments in Seminole Heights.

This neighborhood is one of my favorite places to work in all of Tampa. The homes have genuine character, the kind you can’t replicate in a Wesley Chapel new build no matter how hard you try. But that character comes with complexity, and whoever handles your window treatments needs to know how to deal with it.

Why Off-the-Shelf Won’t Cut It Here

The big box stores sell window treatments in standard sizes. A 36-inch blind. A 48-inch shade. A 72-inch panel. And if your windows happen to be those exact dimensions, great. You’ll save some money and get a product that fits reasonably well.

But Seminole Heights homes weren’t built to modern standard dimensions. Craftsman bungalows from the 1910s through 1940s were built by hand, with hand-cut window frames, and the builders weren’t working from a standardized spec sheet. Windows are odd widths. Headers are slightly uneven. Some casings are deeper than others because different carpenters worked on different rooms.

I’ve seen homeowners in this neighborhood try to make stock blinds work, and the results are always the same. Gaps on one side. Blinds that are slightly too narrow and show light around the edges. Mounting brackets that don’t sit flush because the window casing isn’t perfectly square. It looks off, and in a home with this much architectural character, “off” really stands out.

Custom-measured window treatments are genuinely necessary here. Not as an upsell, not as a luxury, but as a practical requirement to get products that actually fit these windows correctly.

Plantation Shutters and Historic Character

There’s a reason plantation shutters are the most popular product I install in Seminole Heights. They look like they belong in these homes. Craftsman bungalows have that clean-lined, substantial aesthetic, wide trim, exposed rafters, built-in shelving, and shutters share that same DNA. They add architectural weight to the windows rather than just covering them.

For Seminole Heights specifically, I recommend composite shutters from Norman or LuxeHive. The Craftsman aesthetic calls for a 3.5-inch louver, which matches the proportions of the original window trim better than the wider 4.5-inch option. Some homeowners go for the 2.5-inch louver to get closer to the look of original interior shutters from the era, and I respect that choice, even though the narrower louvers give you slightly less view-through when open.

Color matters in these homes too. Bright white shutters can look too modern and sterile against the warm wood tones and earth-colored walls that Seminole Heights homeowners tend to favor. I usually recommend an off-white or warm white, sometimes even a custom color match to the existing trim. Norman offers an extensive color library, and LuxeHive can do custom paint matches, which is invaluable when you’re working with a 1930s home that has cream-colored trim that doesn’t match any standard swatch.

A preservation-minded couple on Hanna Avenue hired me last year after they’d spent months restoring their 1934 bungalow. Original hardwood floors, refinished. Period-appropriate lighting. They even tracked down matching door hardware from a salvage yard in Ybor City. When it came to window treatments, they were terrified of putting something in that would clash with everything they’d worked so hard to restore. We did composite shutters in a warm ivory with 2.5-inch louvers, and the result was stunning. The shutters looked like they could have been original to the house. That couple sent me six referrals in their neighborhood over the following three months.

Roman Shades for the Art-Forward Crowd

Seminole Heights has an artistic, creative energy that sets it apart from most Tampa neighborhoods. The Saturday morning market, the craft breweries, the murals on Nebraska Avenue. The residents here tend to have strong aesthetic opinions and they’re not afraid to make bold choices in their homes.

Roman shades are the window treatment that lets you bring that personality to your windows. Unlike shutters, which are architectural and neutral, Roman shades introduce fabric, texture, pattern, and color. A flat-fold Roman shade in a rich linen looks sophisticated in a dining room. A relaxed Roman shade in a bold geometric print can be the statement piece in an eclectic living room.

Hunter Douglas makes a beautiful Roman shade line with their Design Studio collection. The fabric options are extensive, and the construction quality is top tier. You can also work with Horizons or Graber for excellent Roman shades at a more moderate level. For Seminole Heights homes specifically, I gravitate toward natural-fiber fabrics, linens, and cottons in earthy tones. They complement the Craftsman palette without competing with it.

One practical note: Roman shades stack up when raised, which means they take up some of your window height. In Seminole Heights homes where the windows aren’t always tall to begin with, this matters. A standard Roman shade stacks about 6-8 inches when fully raised. If your window is only 48 inches tall, you’re losing a meaningful chunk of glass. Outside-mount Roman shades can solve this by mounting above the window casing, so the shade stacks against the wall above the glass rather than inside the window frame. It’s a small detail, but in homes where every inch of natural light matters, it makes a difference.

Natural Woven Shades: The Seminole Heights Sweet Spot

If I had to pick one product that captures the Seminole Heights vibe perfectly, it’s natural woven shades. Bamboo, jute, matchstick, rattan. These shades bring warmth and texture that pairs beautifully with the wood-heavy interiors of Craftsman bungalows.

Graber has a fantastic natural shade collection, and their Tradewinds line gives you about forty weave options ranging from very tight (almost like a roller shade) to very open (more decorative than functional). For a Seminole Heights bedroom, go tight weave with a privacy liner. For a kitchen or living room where you want filtered light and texture, a medium weave without a liner gives you that beautiful dappled sunlight effect.

Pricing on natural woven shades is approachable too. A standard window on the weave and whether you add a liner. That’s significantly less than shutters, and the aesthetic might suit your home even better.

Dealing with Those Tricky Windows

Every Seminole Heights home has at least one window that makes me pause during the measure. The arched transom in the front gable. The tiny bathroom window that’s 20 inches wide and mounted 5 feet up. The pair of casement windows flanking the fireplace that swing inward. The jalousie windows in the Florida room that some previous owner added in the 1960s.

These are the windows where experience matters. Here’s how I typically handle the unusual ones.

Arched windows. A custom-shaped shutter panel that follows the arch is the cleanest look, but it’s expensive, often just for the arch portion. A more practical option is to leave the arch uncovered and mount a flat shade or shutter on the rectangular portion below. The arch becomes a design feature on its own. Many Seminole Heights homeowners prefer this approach because it lets the original millwork stay visible.

Casement windows. These swing open on a hinge, so inside-mount treatments need to clear the crank hardware and the swing path. Cellular shades mounted tight to the glass work well here. So do café-style shutters that cover only the lower half of the window, leaving the upper portion and the operating hardware accessible.

Jalousie windows. Honestly? If you still have jalousie windows, the best window treatment is replacing the actual window. They’re energy hogs and security risks. But if replacement isn’t in range right now, a roller shade mounted outside the window frame gives you privacy and some insulation while hiding the dated glass.

Groupings of mismatched windows. This is where Seminole Heights homes get tricky. You might have three windows on one wall that are all slightly different widths. The key is making the treatments look consistent from inside the room, even if the actual dimensions vary. Same product, same color, same mounting height. The slight size differences become invisible when everything else matches.

Preservation-Friendly Installations

A lot of Seminole Heights is designated as a historic district, and even outside the official boundaries, homeowners in this area tend to be protective of their home’s original features. Rightfully so.

I always discuss mounting options carefully in these homes. Drilling into original wood window casings from the 1920s isn’t something I take lightly. Inside-mount installations fit within the window frame and don’t touch the surrounding trim at all, which is ideal for preservation. Outside-mount requires drilling into the casing or the wall above, but the holes are small and easily patched if the treatments are ever removed.

For homeowners who absolutely don’t want any new holes in their original trim, there are tension-mount options for some shade products. They hold by pressure against the inside of the window frame. They’re not as secure as a proper bracket mount, but for lightweight cellular shades or roller shades, they work fine and leave zero trace.

Investing in the Character

Seminole Heights homes are special. They have history and soul that newer construction simply cannot replicate. The window treatments you choose should honor that character while solving the practical problems of living in a century-old Florida home. Cheap, off-the-shelf products stuck into non-standard windows look exactly like what they are. Custom solutions measured to fit your specific home look intentional and permanent, like they were always part of the plan.
